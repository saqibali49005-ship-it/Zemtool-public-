What the Calculator Does

It converts real load power to current when needed, applies material and temperature assumptions, and evaluates AWG/KCMIL or metric candidates. You can find a minimum size, calculate voltage drop for an existing conductor, or estimate the maximum one-way cable distance.

Useful for Solar & Inverters

DC mode is suited to planning runs such as solar-array wiring, battery-to-inverter cables and other low-voltage DC loads. One-way length is doubled internally for the outgoing and return conductors.

Formula

R(T) = K × ρ20 × [1 + α20(T − 20)] / A

DC and single-phase use a 2× path factor; balanced three-phase uses √3. AC drop can optionally include a geometry-based reactance estimate: ΔV ≈ I × L × path factor × (R × PF + X × sinφ), divided by parallel sets. With reactance off, X = 0.

Calculation References & Model Assumptions

The selectable reference profiles use published calculation methods and material references. Their constants are shown so you can compare assumptions; none of these references makes this page a cable approval or code-compliance tool.

  • Default annealed-copper reference: the NIST Copper Wire Tables list a 20°C volume resistivity of 0.017241 Ω·mm²/m and a resistance temperature coefficient of 0.00393/°C. Read the NIST Copper Wire Tables (PDF).
  • Paige reference profile: Paige publishes K factors of 11.2 for copper and 17.4 for aluminium at 127°F (53°C). Read Paige’s wire-size instructions and equations.
  • Omni published-copper profile: Omni publishes a 20°C copper resistivity of 0.0168 Ω·mm²/m and a temperature coefficient of 0.00404/°C. This profile is copper-only because that article does not provide a numerical aluminium reference. Read Omni’s wire-size methodology.

The default EC 1350 aluminium value is a generic reference model, not a guarantee for every alloy, temper or cable product. For a specific cable, use its manufacturer’s resistance and reactance data at the actual operating conditions. The manual R/X option accepts user-supplied values; ZemTools does not independently verify them.

Why can wire-size calculators give different results?

Two calculators can show different voltage drops or minimum conductor sizes even when the visible inputs look identical. Compare the calculation basis before treating a different result as an error.

  • Conductor reference: copper and aluminium resistance values vary by material specification, alloy, temper, stranding and the source's reference temperature. A generic resistivity model may not match a particular cable datasheet.
  • Temperature and construction: resistance rises with conductor temperature. Solid-wire assumptions, stranded-conductor factors and the temperature used by the source can change the result.
  • Length and circuit path: some tools ask for one-way distance while others ask for total conductor length. DC and single-phase models include the outgoing and return path; balanced three-phase uses a different path relationship.
  • AC impedance: power factor and conductor reactance can matter on larger or longer AC runs. A resistance-only estimate may differ from a cable-specific R/X calculation.
  • What the tool is selecting: a voltage-drop-only candidate is not the same as a conductor selected after ampacity, installation, terminal and locally adopted code checks.

For a reproducible comparison, match the circuit type, source voltage, design current, one-way length, material, conductor temperature, voltage-drop target and calculation reference. ZemTools keeps its IACS reference, Paige's published K-factor profile, Omni's published copper profile and user-entered manufacturer R/X data separate rather than blending their assumptions.

One published-profile example illustrates why that matters: for 120 V single-phase, 25 A, 100 m one-way, copper at 50°C and a 3% target, the Omni-published copper model selects 3 AWG, while the default ZemTools IACS model selects 2 AWG. The difference follows their published resistance constants at this borderline size. Check the actual cable data and model assumptions for the project; neither result is an ampacity or installation approval.

How to use this wire size calculator

  1. Choose the circuit type. Select DC, single-phase AC or balanced three-phase AC so the calculator uses the matching current and circuit-path relationship.
  2. Enter the load. Use the design current when it is known. You can also enter power; for AC calculations, provide the power factor when it applies.
  3. Enter system voltage. Use the nominal voltage for the cable run. For a solar DC run, use the voltage relevant to that actual run rather than assuming every solar circuit is the same.
  4. Enter one-way cable length. Measure the physical distance from the source to the load. Do not enter the combined outgoing-and-return length; the calculator applies its path factor.
  5. Select material and voltage-drop target. Choose copper or aluminum and a target suitable for your design brief. A smaller permitted voltage drop can require a larger conductor.

Choose one of three tasks: find a minimum size, calculate drop for a conductor you already have, or calculate the maximum one-way run for that conductor. You can enter the target as a percentage or volts, and model equal-sharing parallel sets. For AC, the optional reactance estimate requires actual conductor spacing and frequency. After calculating, separately check ampacity, protection, installation conditions and applicable electrical requirements before specifying a cable.

Worked example: 120 V single-phase, 20 A

For the stated model assumptions—100 ft one-way, copper, 75°C, Class B/C stranded estimate, PF 1.00 and a 3% voltage-drop target—the smallest passing U.S. candidate is 8 AWG.

Low-voltage example: 12 V DC, 15 A

For a 20 ft one-way run using copper at 75°C, the Class B/C stranded estimate and a 3% target, the default ZemTools IACS model selects 6 AWG with approximately 2.45% voltage drop.

At 12 V, even a modest current can make cable length and voltage-drop target important. This example is a voltage-drop calculation only; it does not select a fuse or verify battery fault current, ampacity, terminals or installation requirements.

What this voltage-drop estimate does not model

The generic resistance calculation applies a first-order temperature correction. The optional AC reactance estimate uses user-supplied geometry; it is not manufacturer-specific impedance data. The calculator does not calculate NEC ampacity, motor-table sizing, continuous-load multipliers, motor starting or locked-rotor current, short-circuit withstand, protective-device ratings or installation-specific ampacity corrections. Its optional ampacity screen only compares final per-conductor limits supplied by the user; it does not generate or validate those limits.

Those checks can change the required cable size. Use the cable manufacturer's data, the equipment instructions and the electrical code adopted for the installation. Where the design is safety-critical, have the selection checked by a qualified electrician or electrical designer.

Wire-size result: what to verify next

The recommendation on this page is a voltage-drop planning result based on the values entered. It should not be treated as a final cable specification or as proof that a conductor can safely carry the load.

Before selecting a cable

  • Check the conductor's allowable ampacity for its material, insulation temperature rating, installation method and ambient conditions.
  • Apply required adjustment and correction factors for grouped conductors and environmental conditions.
  • Confirm terminal temperature limitations, overcurrent protection, short-circuit requirements, grounding and bonding.
  • Use the manufacturer's datasheet for the exact cable construction and the locally adopted electrical code.

For a cable-specific estimate, consult the exact manufacturer’s technical datasheet and confirm that its resistance and reactance values match the operating temperature, frequency and installation. Different models can use different assumptions, so compare the stated calculation basis rather than only the displayed result.

For installations governed by the US NEC, consult the edition adopted by the authority having jurisdiction and the official NFPA 70 information. This calculator does not replace a qualified electrical design review.
